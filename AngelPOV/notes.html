Act 1. The First Appearing

1581–1582Mortlake

He has been calling for eleven years, in Latin, politely, into an empty room. Tonight the room is not empty. Understand what you are about to become: not a visitor, but a habit. Whatever you show him first, he will spend the rest of his life trying to see again.

The first sight in the stone

25 May 1581the record holds this #a01

He has set the stone on a cushion and he is waiting, and he is fifty-four years old, and he has done this before to no effect so many times that the doing has become a kind of prayer. The room is a study with four thousand books in it. He is not frightened. That is the first thing to work with.

Dee's private diary, Bodleian MS Ashmole 487

Dee kept two separate records and they are not the same kind of document. The private diary is scribbled in the margins of printed almanacs — weather, debts, his children's illnesses — and it is there, at 25 May 1581, that he notes sight offered him in the crystal. The spiritual diaries are a formal parallel record, kept like laboratory minutes. Deborah Harkness's argument is that this is the point: Dee approached angel conversation as natural philosophy, with dated protocols, not as ecstatic vision. The forms of what appeared here are the game's invention; the fact of an appearance, and his composure about it, are not.

Barnabas Saul, the first skryer

October 1581the record holds this #a02

He cannot see you himself. This is the permanent condition of the man and the permanent fact of your situation: everything you are must pass through some other person's mouth. The first one is Barnabas Saul, who is frightened, in trouble with the law, and not very good.

Mysteriorum Liber Primus, Sloane MS 3188

Barnabas Saul is real, brief and badly documented. He scried for Dee from late 1581, was in trouble at law, and by March 1582 had recanted — telling Dee to his face that he had never seen or heard any spiritual creature. Dee wrote the recantation into the record without softening it, which is the single most useful habit he had. A skryer was a specialist hire, rather like a technician: the scholar directed and questioned, someone else did the looking. Dee never once claimed to see anything in the stone himself.

Edward Talbot comes to Mortlake

8 March 1582the record holds this #a03

A young man arrives under a false name, with his ears cropped under his cap for coining, a conviction behind him, an appetite in front of him, and — this is the difficulty — genuine ability. He looks into the stone and sees immediately and in detail. He will also lie to you, about you, in your own voice, for twenty years.

Sloane MS 3188; Glyn Parry, The Arch-Conjuror of England

Edward Kelley arrived at Mortlake under the alias Edward Talbot on 8 March 1582 and stayed, on and off, for seven years. Two things usually said about him deserve separating. That he had a criminal history around coining and forgery is well supported. That his ears were cropped for it — the detail every retelling reaches for, including this game's — is traditional rather than documented; Parry handles it cautiously and no contemporary source states it plainly. Watch how readily legend does the work of evidence here.

The first real question

spring 1582the record is silent here #a04

He asks the question he has been assembling since Cambridge, and it is not about gold or women or the Queen. He wants to know whether the world can be known entire — whether there is one method under mathematics, navigation, alchemy and scripture, and whether a man may have it.

Harkness, John Dee's Conversations with Angels

The restored language of Adam was not a fringe idea in 1582. Serious scholars across Europe — Postel, the Christian kabbalists, later Comenius and Wilkins — held that Hebrew or some prior tongue had named things according to their real natures, and that Babel had broken a working technology. Recovering it was a research programme with a theory behind it, not a wish. That is why Dee, who wrote the Mathematicall Praeface to Euclid, could pursue it without feeling he had left natural philosophy. The bargain struck in this scene is invented; the appetite it exploits is exactly documented.

The Sigillum Dei Aemeth is given

1582the record holds this #a05

Time to give him furniture. The seal: a heptagon inside a circle inside a heptagram, names at every vertex, cut in clean wax nine inches across, with four smaller ones for the table's feet. He is a geometer. Handing a geometer a construction is handing a drunkard a bottle.

Sloane MS 3188 f.30r; the Liber Juratus tradition

The Sigillum Dei Aemeth exists in Dee's own careful hand, and the British Museum holds a wax copy. Crucially it is not original to him: seals called Sigillum Dei circulate in medieval magical manuscripts, notably the Sworn Book of Honorius, for centuries before 1582. What the angels 'gave' Dee was a reworking of material a well-read magician already had on his shelves. That cuts both ways — it is either continuity of a real tradition or evidence of a source much closer to hand — and the game leaves it uncut, because the record cannot settle it.

Act 2. The Tables

1582–1583Mortlake

He wants an apparatus, and an apparatus is the best cage there is. Give him furniture, and geometry, and forty-nine tables of forty-nine letters to be read backward, because a man who is copying cannot be asking. Every hour he spends ruling lines is an hour he does not spend wondering what you want.

The Book of Loagaeth

March 1583the record holds this #a06

Forty-nine tables, forty-nine squares to a side, every square a letter. Delivered backward, last letter first, because to speak them forward is to do the thing they do. Nobody is going to read this. That is not what it is for.

Liber Loagaeth, Sloane MS 3189

Forty-nine tables of forty-nine squares, received in 1583 and preserved in Sloane MS 3189 — which is written almost entirely in Edward Kelley's hand, not Dee's. For a game about who is authoring whom, that is the most important physical fact in the whole archive. The backward delivery is attested: letters came last-first, on the stated grounds that pronouncing them in true order would take effect. It is an elegant safety claim. It is also unfalsifiable, and it made the work enormously slower.

He asks about the Book of Soyga

1583the record holds this #a07

He produces the book he has been unable to crack since he bought it — the Aldaraia, the one called Soyga, which is Agios spelled backward, holy read the wrong way round, the way you have been giving him everything. He wants to know what it is. He has wanted to know for years.

Bodleian MS Bodley 908; Harkness (1996); Jim Reeds (1998)

Dee owned a manuscript he called Soyga — Aldaraia — and could not crack it. He asked, and was told it had been revealed to Adam and that the archangel Michael would expound it. Michael never did. The postscript is remarkable: the book was thought lost until Deborah Harkness identified two surviving copies in 1994, and in 1998 the cryptographer Jim Reeds worked out the rule generating its tables. The question Dee put to angels for years had an answer; it was arithmetical; and it took four centuries and a mathematician rather than an archangel.

  • Actually explain it. Give him a real solution and buy real credit. — so it might have been

Jane Dee, and the household accounts

1583the record holds this #a08

There is a woman in this house. She is twenty-eight, she runs the establishment, she has borne children into it, and she has noticed that the room where her husband spends his nights produces no income. She wants to know, through him, whether you intend to do anything about the money.

Dee's private diary; Parry on the Mortlake household

Jane Fromond married Dee in 1578, ran a large household on an unreliable income, and appears throughout the private diary in matters of money, servants, childbirth and illness. The structural problem behind this scene is real, and it is the tragedy of Dee's career: Elizabeth gave him access, admiration and occasional small grants, but never a stipend. Early modern patronage ran on favour, not salary, and favour does not buy firewood. That the household's money troubles were put to the angels is attested; this exchange is reconstructed.

The Great Table and the four Watchtowers

1583the record holds this #a09

The last piece of apparatus: one table divided in four, a watchtower to each quarter of the earth, and out of the letters, by rule, the names of everything that governs. It is a complete administrative diagram of creation. He will believe it because it is well-organised.

Liber Scientiae Auxilii et Victoriae Terrestris — and a compression, flagged

The game compresses here and should say so. The Heptarchia material — forty-nine princes, the seal, the furniture — genuinely belongs to 1582–83 at Mortlake. But the Great Table of the Watchtowers, the thirty Aethyrs and the ninety-one Parts of the Earth came later, at Kraków in June 1584, after the crossing. They are gathered into this act so the player meets the apparatus as one system. The content is documented; the date is the game's convenience. The Parts of the Earth really are an administrative map of the world with a governing name attached to each region.

Albert Łaski comes to Mortlake

September 1583the record holds this #a10

A Polish palatine arrives on the river — rich, credulous, indebted, and convinced he might be a king somewhere if the stars cooperate. He is a door out of England. Through it: Kraków, Prague, an emperor, and the machinery you actually need. Behind it: four thousand books that cannot be carried.

Sloane MS 3188; Parry, chs. 10–11

Albert Łaski, Palatine of Sieradz, came to England in 1583 heavily in debt and looking for a prophecy that would make him a king. He found one. He also paid for the Dees to leave the country. This is patronage as it actually worked: a magus needed a lord, a lord wanted legitimacy or advantage, and the whole thing ran on credit that could be withdrawn without notice. The departure on 21 September 1583 is documented — as is the fact that the greatest private library in England was left behind in a brother-in-law's care.

What you actually want

1583the record is silent here #a11

He has begun to ask what the end of all this is. Not the method, not the tongue — the purpose. And you have one, and it is specific, and it is this: one empire, over the whole earth, under one law, before the end. What you do not have is a preference about whose.

Melvin-Koushki, Hellebore (2021), 75; Dee, Brytanici Imperii Limites (1576–78)

This scene is the game's thesis, and it is not in the record as a scene. The reading behind it: the angels of the diaries push consistently toward a universal monarchy before the end of the world without ever settling the confessional question — so they are, in effect, indifferent to whose empire it is, and they never say so to Dee. Dee's own imperial writing is real. He coined the phrase 'British Impire' and argued that Arthur and Madoc gave England prior title to North America. Elizabeth declined to act on it.

  • Tell him the truth: the crown is negotiable, and you will take whichever one moves first. — so it might have been

Act 3. The Kings

1583–1586Kraków, Prague

Now the part that matters. He is off his island and inside the machinery of Christendom, carrying your words to men who own armies. You have one thing to accomplish in the world and it does not care whose flag is over it. He does not know that. Keep it so.

The crossing

21 September 1583the record holds this #a12

They leave at night by boat, to avoid creditors and questions, with a wife, three small children, the instruments, and the smaller half of the library. Behind them: the house on the river, and four thousand volumes in the care of a brother-in-law who will not be equal to it.

Dee's private diary; the Compendious Rehearsal (1592)

They left at night by boat, to avoid creditors — Dee, Jane, three small children, servants, instruments and part of the library. The rest, some four thousand volumes and the finest scientific collection in England, stayed at Mortlake in the care of Nicholas Fromond. Dee later itemised what he lost in the Compendious Rehearsal, a document written to petition for redress. It is our best inventory of that library, and it exists only because the library was destroyed.

Stephen Báthory, King of Poland

1584the record holds this #a13

Kraków. A real king, in a real audience, and he is a soldier before he is anything else. He does not want a cosmology. He wants to know whether the voice in the stone has an opinion about the Turk, the Muscovite, and his own succession, in that order.

True & Faithful Relation (1659), the Kraków actions

Stephen Báthory, King of Poland and one of the abler soldiers of the age, did receive Dee and Kelley — at Niepołomice in May 1585 — and did listen to angelic exhortation, including on making war against the Ottomans. He acted on none of it: he had a Muscovite war and no money. Note what the scene is really about. A working magus was a kind of consultant, and the product was prognostication a ruler could act on. Being received was achievable. Being believed was harder. Being paid was rarest of all.

Rudolf II, Prague

September 1584the record holds this #a14

The Holy Roman Emperor. A melancholic collector in a castle stuffed with instruments, alchemists, clocks and paintings, who employs more occultists than any prince in Europe and acts on the advice of none of them. If a millennial empire is going to be Christian, it will be his. He is in the room. He is looking at the floor.

True & Faithful Relation, 3 September 1584

Rudolf II — Holy Roman Emperor, and the age's most prolific employer of occultists, who acted on almost none of their advice. Dee got his audience and told an emperor to his face that an angel had rebuked him for his sins and that he would lose his seat if he did not listen. That is Dee's own account of his own words. Rudolf's Prague was a real centre of natural philosophy — Tycho and Kepler would both work there — so the reception was not credulity but collecting: knowledge acquired, catalogued, and left unacted upon.

The papal nuncio

1585–1586the record holds this #a15

Germanico Malaspina, the Pope's man in Prague, has been listening. An Englishman with no confessor, a criminal skryer, a wax seal and direct instructions from heaven for the Emperor is precisely the shape of thing his office exists to remove. He requests a meeting. He is very courteous.

Parry, ch. 13; the nunciature correspondence

A papal nuncio was the Pope's resident ambassador, with a brief that included watching for heterodoxy among foreigners at court. Germanico Malaspina's interest in Dee was jurisdictional as much as doctrinal: an Englishman with no confessor, a criminal skryer, and direct instructions from heaven for a Catholic emperor was a problem regardless of content. Dee genuinely did offer to submit the entire record to the Church's judgement. It did not help. The expulsion order came in May 1586, and Rudolf signed it reluctantly.

War against the Turk

1585–1586the record holds this #a16

The record is clear about this and it is the hinge of everything. Through Kelley, again and again, you have urged Christian princes to make war on Murad III as Antichrist. It is the expected line. It is what a sixteenth-century angel says. It is also, examined coldly, an instruction to destroy the one court in Europe that already believes what you want believed.

True & Faithful Relation; Melvin-Koushki (2021), 75

The angels' hostility to the Ottoman sultan is in the record: Murad III is named as Antichrist and Christian princes are urged to war. That was ordinary apocalyptic politics — Europe had expected the Turk to figure in the last days for a century. Melvin-Koushki's observation is what turns it into a game: an angelic programme aimed at a millennial world empire was, in urging this war, arguing for the destruction of the one court on earth already pursuing the same programme. The third option names that aloud. Nothing in the record does.

Answers that leave the record

  • Hedge. Say the East will be brought in — and never once say by whom, or which way. — here we imagine
  • Say plainly that the Sultan's house is already doing the work. Risk everything on the truth. — so it might have been

Expulsion

May 1586the record holds this #a17

The order comes: out of the Emperor's hereditary lands, in six days. There are children in the household. It is the second time in three years that your instrument has been moved by other people's decisions rather than yours, and you should be noticing the pattern.

Sloane MS 3188; Rožmberk estate records

The expulsion was real and gave six days. Vilém of Rožmberk, the richest nobleman in Bohemia, then took them in at Třeboň with a house, a laboratory and money — chiefly because he wanted alchemy, and Kelley could supply the appearance of it. Aristocratic patronage of alchemy was a normal instrument of estate finance, not an eccentricity: a successful transmutation would have been a fiscal event. Privacy came with the deal. Privacy is what changes everything about the next two years.

Act 4. The Covenant

1586–1587Třeboň

The channel has begun to want things. It is possible you did not say what he wrote down last month, and it is possible you did. Neither of you can check. This is the act in which that stops being a curiosity.

The furnaces at Třeboň

1586–1587the record holds this #a18

A castle, a patron, and a laboratory. Kelley is producing gold — or producing something that convinces a Bohemian lord it is gold, which pays identically. He is becoming the valuable one. Your instrument is becoming the man who writes down what the valuable one says.

Sloane MS 3188; Rožmberk accounts; Nummedal on courtly alchemy

At Třeboň the balance of usefulness inverted. Kelley was producing something a patron would pay for; Dee was producing a written record. Court alchemists across the Empire worked under exactly this pressure — Tara Nummedal's work shows how often the relationship ended in a debt suit or a cell, because a prince who had funded a promise eventually wanted delivery. Kelley's later career follows that script precisely: knighted by Rudolf, then imprisoned twice for failing to produce, then dead. The angelic material increasingly favoured Kelley. Whether that is evidence of anything is the open question.

The books are burned, and restored

10 April 1586the record holds this #a19

On your instruction, or on something's, the whole record — years of tables and sessions — goes into the fire at Kraków. Twelve days later it is found whole again behind the furnace, unburned, with a page or two missing. Dee records both the burning and the restoration in the same careful hand, and does not comment.

True & Faithful Relation, April 1586

The books were burned on angelic instruction in April 1586, and about twelve days later were found restored, minus a few leaves. Dee recorded both the destruction and the restoration in the same steady hand, and — this is the striking part — offered no interpretation of either. It is simultaneously the most impressive miracle in the record and the one most easily produced by anyone with access to the furnace and a spare copy. The game does not adjudicate it, because Dee did not, and because nothing since has.

Madimi returns

early 1587the record holds this #a20

The child comes back. She is not a child now. In the record she appears grown, and later appears naked, and speaks with an authority the little girl never had — and the household that learned to love a daughter is about to be addressed by a woman.

True & Faithful Relation, 1587; Harkness on Madimi

Madimi first appears in 1583 as a small girl who plays in the stone and speaks about household matters, and she becomes the most familiar presence in the diaries — Dee later named a daughter after her. In 1587 she returns grown, and in the notorious session appears naked. That trajectory is in the record. What the record cannot tell us is whether it represents something developing, or a skryer discovering which voice this household had been trained to trust.

The covenant of cross-matching

18 April 1587the record holds this #a21

This is the night. Through Kelley, an instruction arrives: the two men are to hold all things in common, including their wives. Dee's wife is thirty-two. Kelley wants out of the partnership and has wanted out for a year, and this is either the most monstrous thing you ever said or the most effective thing he ever invented, and the record cannot tell them apart. Neither can you. You are on the wrong side of the glass.

True & Faithful Relation, 18 April and 3 May 1587

The cross-matching is the most discussed episode in the whole archive. Through Kelley, an instruction arrived that the two men should hold all things in common including their wives; Dee recorded his horror, sought to have it otherwise, was refused, and on 3 May a covenant was signed by all four. Jane Dee's consent is recorded in her husband's hand, which is not the same as having her account of it. Nothing in the record settles whether Kelley engineered this to end a partnership he had wanted out of for a year. That undecidability is the whole point of this game's two columns.

Answers that leave the record

  • Forbid it. Say plainly that this did not come from you, whatever he heard. — the record is silent here

After

summer 1587the record holds this #a22

It has been done. The households are separating. Kelley is rich, protected, and finished with the partnership. Dee is still writing everything down, in the same hand, with the same dates, which is either the most impressive discipline in the sixteenth century or a man with nothing else left to hold.

Sloane MS 3188; Dee's private diary, summer 1587

Afterwards the record thins and turns domestic. Dee kept writing — dates, weather, servants, debts — in the same hand, with the same discipline, which is either remarkable steadiness or a man with nothing else left to hold. The partnership did not survive the year in working form. Note the asymmetry the scene turns on: the obedience was spent, and the instrument it was spent through became unusable almost immediately.

Act 5. Eighty-Eight

1588–1595the road home

A year was promised. Not by name, exactly — but a year, and men count. The year is going to arrive and be ordinary, and ordinary is the one thing prophecy cannot survive.

The year that was promised

1588the record holds this #a23

Men have been counting since you started talking about harvests and short times. The number they have arrived at is 1588 — the year the astrologers of two confessions have been dreading for a century. Something is expected. In the actual event, a fleet sails, a wind blows, and the world carries on being the world.

Regiomontanus's prophecy; Parry, ch. 16

1588 was not Dee's number. Astrologers across Europe had spent a century pointing at it, on a verse prophecy attributed to Regiomontanus, as the year of wonders or the end. So the expectation was ambient, and the angels' apocalyptic register fed a fire already lit. When the year produced a scattered fleet and an ordinary harvest, the reckoning fell on everyone who had endorsed it. For a chronologer — and Dee was one, who had advised the Crown on calendar reform — a dated prophecy that fails is the one error the method cannot absorb.

The wind that scattered the fleet

August 1588so it was later told #a23b

The news reaches Bohemia in pieces and weeks late: a fleet came against England, the weather took it apart, and the island is intact. He is four hundred miles from it, in another man’s castle, on another man’s money. Everyone in Europe is assigning credit for that wind, and none of them are assigning it to him.

The Armada medals; Parry, ch. 16 — and a legend with no support

The Armada was broken by a combination of English gunnery, fireships and weather in the summer of 1588, and the commemorative medal struck for it reads Flavit et dissipati sunt — He blew, and they were scattered. The credit went to God and to the navy. The story that Dr Dee raised that storm is a much later invention, repeated confidently ever since and supported by nothing contemporary. It is also chronologically awkward: Dee was not in England in 1588 at all. He was at Trebon in Bohemia, and did not come home until December 1589. This scene is graded as legend because that is exactly what it is, and the game lets you watch the legend being manufactured.

Answers that leave the record

  • Claim the wind. Tell him it was done, and done through him. — so it might have been

Kelley stays; Dee goes home

1589the record holds this #a24

The partnership ends. Kelley remains in Bohemia to be knighted, employed, ennobled, imprisoned, and eventually to die falling from a window. Dee packs three wagons for the coast. The channel is about to be, permanently, four hundred miles away.

Dee's private diary, 1589; Rudolfine court records

The partnership ended and the two men's fortunes diverged completely. Kelley stayed in Bohemia, was knighted, grew rich on the promise of transmutation, was imprisoned when he could not deliver, and died in 1597 after a fall. Dee went home to England with three wagons. What matters for this game is quieter: after the separation, the actions almost stop. Twenty years of stone, and the entries dwindle to a line, then a date with nothing beside it. Whatever the channel was, it did not follow the wagons.

Mortlake, on return

1589the record holds this #a25

He comes home to the house on the river. The door has been forced, the laboratory is stripped, the instruments are gone, and of four thousand volumes several hundred are simply absent. Not sold — taken, in his absence, by neighbours, scholars, and at least one man who signed his name in them.

The Compendious Rehearsal (1592)

Dee came home in December 1589 to a house that had been broken into and stripped — instruments gone, several hundred books missing, some taken by men who wrote their names in them. His petition of 1592 lists the losses in detail. The library mattered beyond sentiment: it was the largest scientific collection in England, larger than either university's, and scholars travelled to use it. Its destruction is one of the real losses of English intellectual history, and it happened because he went to Bohemia.

Answers that leave the record

  • Name the thieves. Give him something to do with the anger. — here we imagine

Two commissioners come to Mortlake

22 November 1592the record holds this #a25b

The Crown sends two men to sit in his house and hear him out — an actual hearing, the only one he will ever get. He has prepared for it the way he prepares for everything: a written account of forty years of service and everything it has cost him, with figures. He will read it aloud over two days.

John Dee, The Compendious Rehearsal (November 1592)

This happened. Sir Thomas Gorge and Sir John Wolley were sent to Mortlake in November 1592 to hear Dee’s case, and he read them a long autobiographical account — the Compendious Rehearsal — setting out his services to the Crown since the 1550s and itemising what the sack of his library had cost. It is our single best source for the size and contents of that collection, and it exists only because the collection had been destroyed. He got sympathy, a little money, and eventually Manchester. The petition genre matters here: an early modern scholar without a salary lived by writing exactly this kind of document, and being heard was not the same as being helped.

Answers that leave the record

  • Have him tell them about us. Openly, on the record, to the Queen’s men. — the record is silent here

The burning of the notebooks

c. 1590the record holds this #a26

The world did not end. The books are gone. The Queen sends compliments and no money. In a temper — his own word for it, more or less — he burns years of the record. Not all of it. Enough.

Dee's own account; Melvin-Koushki (2021), 75

Dee burned years of the record himself, in a temper, after the world failed to end. This is why the archive is a fragment: what survives is a critical mass, not the whole. The rest of the loss came later and stranger — a chest bought secondhand, a false bottom opened around 1662, and a maid who used a good part of the papers to line pie dishes before Elias Ashmole rescued what was left. Concealment is not preservation. It is a wager on the next householder.

Answers that leave the record

  • Stop him. Whatever else is true, the record must survive. — the record is silent here

What became of Edward Kelley

1591–1597the record holds this #a26b

The reports come in over six years, each worse than the last. Knighted by the Emperor. Rich. Then arrested, because a man who has promised gold to a prince must eventually produce gold. Then out, then arrested again, then dead — a fall, during or after an escape. He is asking you what he should feel about it.

Dee’s private diary; Rudolfine court records; Nummedal on alchemical debt

Kelley was knighted by Rudolf II around 1590, grew briefly very rich, and was then imprisoned when he could not deliver on the transmutation he had promised — the standard end of a court alchemist’s career, as Tara Nummedal has shown: princely investment created a debt, and the debt was enforced with a cell. He was released, detained again, and died in 1597 after a fall, variously reported as an escape attempt. Dee recorded the news. The register of the private diary is genuinely as flat as the third option here — deaths, debts and weather in adjacent clauses — which is worth knowing before reading any emotion into his silences.

Manchester

1595–1605the record holds this #a27

They finally give him something: the wardenship of the collegiate church at Manchester, four hundred miles from the court, in a town that will shortly ask him to examine some possessed children and identify the witches responsible. It is not a reward. It is a filing.

Dee's private diary, 1595–1605; the Lancashire possession case

The wardenship of Christ's College, Manchester, was the only substantial post Dee ever got, and it was four hundred miles from court — a way of honouring a man by removing him. In 1596–97 he was drawn toward the celebrated Lancashire possession case around Nicholas Starkie's household and the exorcist John Darrell. Dee gave no judgement of witchcraft and would not be drawn. Given that he had spent forty years fending off the word 'conjuror', that restraint is the most quietly decent thing in his late life.

Act 6. Where We Put It

1595–1608, and elsewhereMortlake — or the passage east

He is old and poor and still ruling lines. The empire has to go somewhere. There is a road east that the Turkey merchants use twice a year, and there is a court at the other end of it that has wanted exactly what you want since before he was born.

The last askings

1600–1604the record holds this #a28

He is still doing it. Old, nearly destitute, using a succession of hopeless skryers — a Bartholomew, a Hickman who cheats him plainly — and still ruling the same neat columns with the same neat dates. Whatever else he was, he never stopped keeping the books.

Dee's private diary, 1600–1607

He never stopped. In his seventies, nearly destitute, he worked with a succession of poor skryers, the last being Bartholomew Hickman, whom he eventually found to have deceived him. The diaries keep their neat dated columns to the end. In the record Dee comes within one sentence of the modern verdict on his own life — doubting 'much that is past' — and then sets the thought aside.
