HISTORIOGRAPHICAL_ISSUES.md — The Ten Arguments About John Dee

A synthesis of the 47 scholarly chapter summaries in the DeeChunks corpus (Parry 21, Szőnyi 9, Sherman 7, Håkansson 6, Clucas 2, Walton 1, Forshaw 1), reorganised by debate rather than by book, with page ranges, and mapped to the game nodes that carry each one.

Raw per-chapter rows live in RESEARCH_INDEX.md §1. This file is the argument-level layer above it: what modern scholars actually disagree about, and what Imperial Magus does with each disagreement.

The governing design rule

Where the scholarship is unresolved, the game does not resolve it — it makes the player enact it. A live historiographical dispute is almost always a decision Dee genuinely faced, seen from the far end. So each debate below becomes a choice, a gate, or an ending condition; none of them becomes a line of narration telling the player who was right.

This is also the practical answer to "how do you make a biography game replayable." The ten debates are ten axes along which two playthroughs can differ and both be defensible history.

1. Isolated magus, or embedded political operator?

The old picture — Dee the solitary Renaissance mage, best known via Yates's Hermetic tradition (in the corpus, and explicitly contested by everyone after her).

Sherman replaces it outright: Dee is a reader-writer operating inside Elizabethan systems of access, dependence, counsel and reputation, and his marginality itself is political — a career reconstructed from failed offices, patronage negotiations and manuscript traces rather than a rise-and-fall (ch. 1, pp. 15–56). His political manuscripts are practical interventions in government, not eccentric side projects (ch. 5, pp. 204–216).

Parry agrees on the embedding and adds the knives: a world already saturated with magical assumption (ch. 1, pp. 23–35), where mathematics and astrology are practical technologies of service (ch. 2, pp. 35–48).

In the game. This is the premise, not a choice — hence a persistent panel of verbs rather than powers, and ACCESS rather than magical strength as the scarce resource. Nodes: d04 (which door into service), d06, d10, d17, d32.

2. Were the angel conversations a collapse, or the natural philosophy continuing?

The oldest and most consequential dispute. The Victorian and early-modern reception read the actiones as credulity or senility. Every scholar in this corpus rejects that.

  • Harkness (the title argument: Cabala, Alchemy, and the End of Nature) — the

sessions are rigorous natural philosophy under eschatological pressure: the Book of Nature is decaying, and an Adamic key is needed to read the cosmos before the end.

  • Szőnyi — Dee's printed natural philosophy is already symbolic magic, so the angel

work is not a break from it (ch. 6, pp. 155–181); the diaries integrate into a theory of exaltation and symbolic communication (ch. 7, pp. 181–241).

  • Håkansson — ritual and natural magic are integrated with theology, language and

eschatology throughout (ch. 5, pp. 240–332).

  • Parry — treats the conversations as a managed project: hierarchy, ritual, and

contested authority (ch. 16, pp. 200–215).

In the game. Act V must not read as a man losing his mind. d21's three options are all methods — pious, methodical, urgent — never "descend into madness." The urgency option carries Harkness's clock explicitly. Nodes: d21, d24, d25, d27c.

3. Is the Monas alchemy, or a universal science of number?

The sharpest technical dispute in the corpus, and the one with the clearest game payoff.

  • Walton (1976) — a geometrical cabala: mathematical figure plus cabalistic

association generating hidden meaning (pp. 1–8).

  • Clucas — pushes against a narrowly alchemical reading: the Monas rests on

Pythagorean arithmology, especially Trithemian number symbolism, and Dee imagined it as a disciplina noua broader than alchemy alone (ch. 1, pp. 1–23).

  • Clucas again — and its meaning was made in the pressroom: Dee, Willem Silvius, and

the diagrammatic/typographical arrangement are active parts of the argument, not packaging (ch. 2, pp. 1–18).

  • Forshaw — settles the "unread English curiosity" question: a substantial continental

alchemical afterlife, in Latin, French and German, through the Theatrum Chemicum, Khunrath, Libavius, and into Rosicrucianism (pp. 1–23).

  • Parry — reads it as kabbalistic-alchemical decoding of creation, tied to court

ambition (ch. 5, pp. 68–81).

In the game. Node d13 is this debate, playable: you compose the glyph in Silvius's house and choose whether it is set linguistic (letters, permutation — the Clucas/Walton reading, which buys the continental afterlife Forshaw documents), pyronomic (proportions, radii — the practical reading an emperor's furnace-master can use), or sealed. d14 then chooses its first reader. Trithemius's presence in Clucas's argument is also the series' most legitimate cross-game thread — see §10.

4. Did the "conjuror" label come from outside or inside?

Parry's central thesis, and the game's spine. Reputation is a historical force, not a by-product (ch. 6, pp. 81–94). It is manufactured and weaponised: alchemy is a social battlefield of expertise, fraud, hope and patronage (ch. 8, pp. 103–114); rivals like Prestall prosper on bolder claims; Murphyn forges letters and runs a slander campaign for two decades (pp. 87–109). Self-defence becomes a central practice of the later career (ch. 11, pp. 138–151). Anti-magic reaction then arrives as an independent historical force (ch. 18, pp. 226–238), sharpened by invasion fear (ch. 19, pp. 238–258) and by the exorcism controversy (ch. 21, pp. 273–322).

Dating correction (from the corpus, 2026-08-31): Murphyn's attack helps explain Dee's failure to secure appointments in the 1560s and later, and Dee complained of "intensifying slanders" in the Mathematical Preface itself, February 1570 (Parry, pp. 89–105). The slander is therefore earlier and longer than a single c.1580 lawsuit. d20 was written as a c.1580 episode and has been re-dated accordingly.

In the game. stain is the only tracked quantity that rises easily and effectively never falls. Nodes: d19 (Prestall — match his promises or refuse the ground), d20 (Murphyn — sue, seek clerical cover, or ignore), d29e, d34m, and the payoff at d35, where the petition to be tried is refused.

5. Was "the occult" even a separate category?

  • Parry — magic is part of ordinary political and religious culture, not an exotic

deviation (ch. 1).

  • Szőnyi — refuses to separate occult philosophy from early modern science, medicine

and prophecy (ch. 5, pp. 105–155), and treats esotericism as a cultural-historical category rather than failed science (ch. 1, pp. 12–29).

  • Håkansson — reads Dee through language history and symbolic exegesis rather than

isolated magical technique (ch. 3, pp. 73–139).

In the game. No scene ever frames Dee as transgressing into "the occult." Astrology is a commission; alchemy is a court industry; the sessions are a research programme. The [capability] labels are period arts, not spell schools. This is a writing-guide rule as much as a design one.

6. What was the Mortlake library for?

Sherman — a living, working institution: acquisition, annotation, consultation, lending, loss and scholarly sociability (ch. 2, pp. 56–80), with the marginalia as the central evidence of intellectual labour — underlining, symbols, corrections, cross-references disclosing habits of selection and argument (ch. 4, pp. 116–204). Reading is a historically specific act, recoverable as evidence (ch. 3, pp. 80–116).

In the game. Two consequences. First, library is a tracked quantity built by choices and spent or scattered by them — the collection is a thing you do, not a number you own. Second, and larger: the entire interface is marginalia. Consequence text is Dee's own second-ink annotation; capability options render as underlinings; the ending is a catalogue. Sherman's argument is the game's visual identity. Nodes: d16, d09 (Bonner's books), d31, d38.

7. Was the imperial programme real policy, or occult ambition in code?

  • Sherman — a political programme built from historical reading, legal argument,

navigation and the rhetoric of British recovery (ch. 7, pp. 277–394); the Synopsis is a structured map of Elizabethan institutions and offices (ch. 6, pp. 216–277).

  • Parry — the imperial writings hide alchemical and occult ambitions inside

political and navigational language; "more is hid than uttered" (ch. 10, pp. 127–138) — empire as a magical recovery project (ch. 9, pp. 114–127).

Both readings are of the same documents. This is the corpus's most genuinely unresolved disagreement.

In the game. d17 is the fork, and neither branch is marked correct: the Limits (Arthur, Madoc, prior title — Parry's recovery project) or the Synopsis (navy, fisheries, wool — Sherman's commonwealth). The empire flag reads at the Imperial Geographer ending, which cites whichever the player actually wrote.

8. Kelley: deceiver, believer, or co-author?

Parry is the most precise: Kelley's rise shifts the balance of power, and Dee's authority breaks down as Kelley gains independent value as an alchemist (ch. 17, pp. 215–226); the partnership is a governed enterprise with contested authority (ch. 16). Szőnyi places Kelly inside the interpretive community that produced Dee's meaning (ch. 8, pp. 241–281). No one in the corpus adjudicates his sincerity.

In the game — the no-ground-truth rule. The game never states whether the angels were real, and therefore never states what Kelley was. Every epistemic choice is doubled: about the message, and about the man. d23 offers confront / suppress / test, and the test passes some and fails others, which is what both a real oracle and a good cheat would produce. Nodes: d22, d23, d27c, d29c, d30c, d28e.

9. Provincial Englishman, or European?

Parry — Louvain training, Mercator, Gemma Frisius, instruments, and court utility (ch. 2). Forshaw — the Monas's continental reception proves the traffic ran outward too. Szőnyi — Central Europe and patronage as constitutive, not an episode (ch. 8).

In the game. d02 and d03 are where the Continent is either bound to Dee or let go, and both feed ottoman — the cultivation score that eventually gates the counterfactual. The d26 foreclosure is the same question asked once, finally, with the library as the stake.

10. Who made the Dee we have?

Szőnyi — Dee's meaning is produced by communities of patrons, readers, polemicists and editors; Casaubon's reception is part of the object (ch. 8). Håkansson — positions current scholarship after the older stories of magic and science (ch. 2, pp. 35–73).

In the game. Every ending closes with a reception coda that varies with how the player treated the record itself: whether the books were burned, sealed, kept exactly, or scattered. Casaubon printing the conversations in order to damn them, and thereby preserving them, is the series' recurring irony — TurkaGame's Zīj-i Shāhjahānī beat in English dress. Nodes: d25, d37, d39, and codaFor() in content/pack.js.

Cross-game note: Trithemius

Clucas's Monas argument runs through Trithemian number symbolism, and Szőnyi's ch. 5 names Trithemius, Agrippa and Paracelsus as the intellectual matrix Dee worked in. This is documented intertextuality among three of the five planned protagonists — the legitimate connective tissue for the series, and the reason the house rule bans invented contact but not shared sources.
